Lesson 01 · Method

The 45-Minute Blueprint

You can build a server that explains itself down to node:http — eight lessons' worth. The interview won't ask you to. It hands you a product name and 45 minutes of fog, then scores how you conduct yourself in the fog. Today: the blueprint that turns the fog into a schedule.

~30 min method spoken

What is actually being scored

Nobody expects you to reproduce Instagram's real production diagram — the interviewers who ask "design Instagram" couldn't either. What they probe is structured thinking under ambiguity: Do you ask before you draw? Do the numbers behind your boxes survive arithmetic? When pushed into one component, do you go deep — or repeat the overview louder? The System Design Primer says it plainly: interviewers evaluate how you solve problems, not what final solution you recite. That is good news: method can be drilled. Memorized diagrams can't be — the follow-up question always finds the edge of the memorization.

The one idea

The interview is scored on method, not on the final drawing — and the method has four beats, each with a time budget. Steal the clock from the interview: roughly 5 minutes of requirements, 5 of estimation, 15 of high-level design, 20 of deep dives. The phases aren't bureaucracy; each one exists to make the next one safe (PDF: ByteByteGo archive p.162 — the System Design Topic Map; p.134 — the Top 20 concepts this course will walk through, one phase at a time).

1 · Requirements — 5′→ 2 · Estimation — 5′→ 3 · High-level — 15′→ 4 · Deep dives — 20′
from step 4 on, the interviewer steers — pick the box they point at
Signal beats completeness: a defensible half-diagram outscores an unexplained full one.
  1. Requirements & scope (5′). Restate the prompt in your own words, then pin down functional scope — the 2–3 core features you'll design — and non-functional scope: how many users (DAU), read/write balance, latency targets, consistency needs. State assumptions out loud and let the interviewer cheaply correct them: "I'll assume 10M DAU, read-heavy — correct me."
  2. Back-of-envelope estimation (5′). From that DAU number derive the orders of magnitude: QPS (read and write separately), storage per day, memory a cache would need. Powers of two, whole numbers, zero precision theater — the point is sanity, not decimal places. Full drill next lesson.
  3. High-level design (15′). Draw the boxes: client → DNS/CDN → load balancer → app servers → database, cache, queue as needed. Then do the move that separates candidates: walk one concrete request through your own diagram, end to end — the same move as your curl → route → SQL walkthroughs, now narrated to a stranger.
  4. Deep dives & trade-offs (20′). The interviewer picks a box and says "go deeper" — sharding? cache invalidation? hot keys? The winning shape is always the same sentence: "I choose X over Y, because here I'm buying ___ at the price of ___." Every design choice trades something for something; naming the trade is the job.

The skeleton, once — URL shortener in four rows

The full walkthrough arrives as a Phase 4 capstone; here it appears once, compressed, to prove the blueprint fits any prompt. Watch the same four beats carry a real answer:

BeatWhat you produce (sketch level)
Requirementstwo features: shorten a long URL, redirect fast; skip accounts/analytics aloud. Assume 10M new links/mo, read-heavy ~100:1.
Estimation~4 writes/s, ~400 reads/s — one modest server would survive today; design for growth, not for the number.
High-levelclient → LB → tiny app tier → unique-ID service (counter + base62) → key-value store; cache for hot links in front.
Deep diveswhy not hash-the-URL (collisions) · 301 vs 302 trade-off · what breaks when one link goes viral (hot key → cache it).

Notice the estimation deliberately proves the system is small — and the design proceeds anyway, because the interviewer is scoring judgment about growth, not today's traffic. That move — "compute it, then design past it" — is worth more than any memorized diagram.

The drill — rehearse the method, on paper, out loud

Hands-on · 15 minutes, paper only
  • Open the archive and read two pages, map-the-terrain style, no note-taking: the System Design Topic Map (p.162) and Top 20 System Design Concepts (p.134) — you're meeting future lessons, not studying them
  • Pick an app you used today (X, Spotify, Uber). Write: 2 functional requirements, 2 non-functional, 1 stated assumption. Three minutes.
  • Four-step skeleton for "design a pastebin": write the four headings, one bullet each. Ten lines maximum — the discipline is the brevity.
  • Say the skeleton out loud in under 3 minutes, phone timer running. The interview is spoken; this muscle is separate from the writing one.
  • Bring friction to the agent (me): where did the skeleton feel arbitrary? Which step felt like stalling? That discomfort is the real syllabus.
