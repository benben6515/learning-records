Free Writing: The Method

~5 min read Solo · Free

Writing shares the same disease as your speaking and your reading: you translate from Chinese first, and you freeze because you edit while you write. The cure is free-writing, and it comes down to one rule. This lesson is the why and the how — Lesson 05 is the daily workout, and because you can send me the draft, it's the tightest feedback loop of all your skills.

Why you freeze when you write (the real reasons)

  1. Translating from Chinese first. Same root as your reading habit (Lesson 02). Every sentence takes two trips: thought → Chinese → English → page. Fluent writers go thought → English → page.
  2. The inner editor. You stop mid-sentence to fix a tense, hunt a better word, delete and rewrite. Each stop breaks the chain. Writing teacher Peter Elbow named this clash — producing and editing are two different brain modes, and mixing them sabotages both.
  3. Dictionary fear. The moment you reach for a translation app, the flow is dead. Don't have the word? Write around it and keep moving.

The fix: free-writing

One rule: keep your fingers moving. Don't edit. Don't translate. Don't look anything up. Don't cross out. An ugly first draft is the goal, not a failure — editing is a separate pass that comes after.

Why it works
  • It silences the inner editor. Speed forces you to stop polishing and start producing. The first draft is supposed to be messy — that's how you outrun the critic.
  • It surfaces gaps (comprehensible output). Writing is where you discover what you can't yet say. Every word you reach for and don't have is a map dot for what to learn next.
  • It trains "think in English". Same mechanism as Lesson 02: skip the Chinese translation trip entirely.
  • It builds the one-sitting muscle. The Mission goal — 150–250 words without a dictionary — is just a sprint you've done a hundred times.

Not folklore: the free-writing method comes from Peter Elbow's Writing Without Teachers (1973) and is now standard practice in writing courses worldwide. Read the method write-up →

The 5-minute rule If 15 minutes feels like too much today, do 5. Five minutes every day will beat two hours every Sunday, every single time. Consistency is the whole game.
