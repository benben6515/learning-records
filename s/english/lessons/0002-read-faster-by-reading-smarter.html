Lesson 02 · Reading Fluency

Read Faster by Reading Smarter

~12 min drill + read Solo · Free

You read dev docs every day, and you've felt the problem: you're slow. The good news — it's not your eyes, and it's fixable. You told me you translate to Chinese as you read. That's two trips for every sentence: English in → Chinese → meaning. No wonder it's slow. And here's the bonus: the same habit is what freezes your speaking. Fix it here and your speech speeds up too.

Why you're slow (the real reasons)

  1. Decoding, not recognising. Every word you only half-know is a speed bump — your brain spells it out instead of knowing it instantly. Researcher Paul Nation found you need to know about 98% of the words on a page to read comfortably without a dictionary (Nation, 2006). So slowness is mostly a vocabulary problem, not a speed problem.
  2. Translating word-by-word. Two trips per sentence. Fluent readers skip the translation.
  3. Reading single words. Your eyes can grab 2–4 words at each stop. If you stop at every word, you read several times slower than you could.

The fix: three habits

  • Read in chunks — let your eyes land on phrase groups ("when you type / a web address"), not single words.
  • Tolerate gaps — don't stop for every unknown word. Guess from context and keep moving.
  • Build sight vocabulary — the more words you recognise instantly, the faster you go.
Two rules that protect your understanding The 3× rule: only look up a word if it appears 3+ times or blocks the whole meaning. Otherwise, keep going.
Read at the right level: if you're looking up words constantly, the text is too hard — drop to an easier one. Easy reading builds speed; hard reading builds frustration.

When you type a web address into your browser and press enter, a lot happens in just one second. First, your browser sends a request to a server. A server is simply another computer, somewhere in the world, that stores the files for the website.

While it waits, the browser receives the page piece by piece. The first piece is the structure — the bones of the page, written in HTML. Once the browser understands the structure, it asks for the styles, the images, and the scripts that make the page look right and feel alive.

This is why a page often appears in stages: first you see plain text, then the layout snaps into place, and finally the colors, fonts, and buttons arrive. Each step depends on the one before it. If one file is slow, everything after it must wait.

Most of this is invisible. You simply watch the page fill in, click a link, and move on — never thinking about the quiet conversation between your browser and a distant machine.
