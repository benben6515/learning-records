THE SOUND OF THE BELL

平　家　物　語　·　祇　園　精　舍

TAIRA NO KIYOMORI · THE GENJI WAR · DAN-NO-URA · 1371

"The sound of the Gion Shōja bells echoes the impermanence of all things. The color of the sala flowers reveals the truth that the prosperous must decline. The proud do not endure, they are like a dream on a spring night. The mighty fall, they are like dust before the wind." So begins the Tale of the Heike — the great Japanese epic of a clan that rose to absolute power, ruled Japan with arrogance, and was destroyed, root and branch, in a war that ended with a child-emperor drowned in the sea. This is the story the bell tells: that all that is beautiful and powerful is also already passing. 「祇園精舍之鐘聲，響諸行無常之響。娑羅雙樹之花色，顯盛者必衰之理。驕者不持久，宛如春夜一夢。猛者終將亡，如同風前之塵。」《平家物語》如此開始——那部偉大的日本史詩，講述一個升至絕對權力、以傲慢統治日本的氏族，在一場以幼帝沉於大海而告終的戰爭中，被連根拔起地毀滅。這便是那口鐘所講述的故事：一切美麗而強大的，也已在消逝之中。

Chapter 1 · 第一章

The Opening Bell

開卷之鐘

The Tale of the Heike begins with a bell — and the bell is the whole story. "The sound of the Gion Shōja bells echoes the impermanence of all things." The Gion Shōja was the monastery of the Buddha himself in India — and its bell, the tradition says, rang at dawn and dusk, and its sound was a reminder that everything is passing. And the Heike begins with that sound, because the Heike IS that sound — it is the story of something that was great and is gone, told by someone who already knows the ending, who begins every sentence with the awareness that all of this — the power, the glory, the beauty — is dust.

《平家物語》以一口鐘開始——而那口鐘便是整個故事。「祇園精舍之鐘聲，響諸行無常之響。」祇園精舍是佛祖本人在印度的精舍——而它的鐘，傳統說，在黎明與黃昏時敲響，而它的聲音是一個提醒：一切都在消逝。而《平家》以那個聲音開始，因為《平家》便是那個聲音——它是一個曾偉大而如今不在之物的故事，由一個已經知道結局的人所講述，他以一切——那權力、那榮耀、那美——皆是塵土的覺知來開始每一句話。

"The proud do not endure, they are like a dream on a spring night. The mighty fall, they are like dust before the wind." These lines are among the most famous in all of Japanese literature, and they are the whole of the Heike in two sentences. The epic does not build toward a climax of surprise — it begins with the certainty of decline, and every page that follows is the slow, beautiful, heartbreaking unfolding of that certainty. We know from the first line that the Taira will fall. We know from the first line that their glory is a dream. And the art of the story is not in the surprise but in the grace — the grace with which the doomed clan walks, page by page, toward the end the bell has already announced.

「驕者不持久，宛如春夜一夢。猛者終將亡，如同風前之塵。」這些詩行是全部日本文學中最著名的之一，而它們便是整部《平家》在兩句話中的全部。這部史詩不構建向一個驚奇的高潮——它以衰落的確定性開始，而其後的每一頁都是那個確定性的緩慢、美麗、令人心碎的展開。我們從第一行就知道平氏將傾。我們從第一行就知道他們的榮耀是一場夢。而故事的藝術不在驚奇，而在那份優雅——那份註定毀滅的氏族一頁一頁地走向那口鐘已然宣告之終局的優雅。

Chapter 2 · 第二章

The Taira's Rise

平氏之興

The Taira — the Heike — were a samurai clan who, under the leadership of Taira no Kiyomori, rose from the ranks of the provincial warriors to become the most powerful family in Japan. And the tradition tells us the boast that defined them at their height: "Anyone who is not a member of the Taira clan is not a human being." The whole government was theirs. The emperor was their puppet — Kiyomori's daughter was the emperor's wife, and her son sat on the throne. The high offices, the provinces, the revenues — all flowed to the Taira. They were, for a brief, shining moment, the undisputed masters of Japan.

平氏——平家——是一個武士氏族，在平清盛的領導之下，從地方武士的行列升為日本最有權勢的家族。而傳統告訴我們那個定義他們在巔峰之時的豪語：「凡非平氏者，皆非人也。」整個政府是他們的。天皇是他們的傀儡——清盛的女兒是天皇之妻，而她的兒子坐在王座上。那些高位、那些行省、那些收入——全部流向平氏。他們，在一個短暫而閃亮的時刻，是日本無可爭議的主人。

And the tradition gives us the Taira at their most magnificent — the processions, the silk robes, the poetry contests, the grand temples they built, the glittering court life they sponsored. And the reader, who has already heard the bell, watches this magnificence with a strange double vision: we see the glory, and we see the dust it will become, simultaneously. The Taira's silk is already the moth's wing. Their poetry is already an elegy. Their temples are already ruins. The opening lines have taught us to see this way — to see the blossom and its falling at once — and the whole epic is an exercise in that double vision. The Taira rise. The Taira shine. And we, who have heard the bell, already mourn them.

而傳統給了我們最輝煌之時的平氏——那些行列、那些綾羅綢緞、那些歌合、他們所建的宏偉寺院、他們所資助的閃亮宮廷生活。而讀者，那個已經聽過鐘聲的，以一種奇異的雙重視覺觀看這份輝煌：我們看見榮耀，而我們同時看見它將成為的塵土。平氏的綾羅已是飛蛾之翅。他們的詩歌已是輓歌。他們的寺院已是廢墟。開卷之辭教了我們這樣看——同時看見花與花落——而整部史詩是那種雙重視覺的練習。平氏升起。平氏閃耀。而我們，那些聽過鐘聲的人，已在為他們哀悼。

Chapter 3 · 第三章

The Arrogance of Power

權力之傲

And the Taira, the tradition tells us, were arrogant. Power had gone to their heads. They dismissed the old courtiers, ignored the protests of the monks, humiliated the retainers who had served them. And Kiyomori, the patriarch, was the worst — a man so drunk on power that, when the monks of a great temple protested his policies, he burned the temple to the ground. When his own son warned him that the clan was making too many enemies, Kiyomori laughed: "If the people hate us, let them hate us. What can they do?" The tradition marks this as the hinge — the moment when the Taira's pride crossed the line from confidence into recklessness, from power into the arrogance that destroys power.

而平氏，傳統告訴我們，是傲慢的。權力上了他們的頭。他們打發了舊朝臣，無視了僧侶的抗議，羞辱了侍奉他們的家臣。而清盛，那位家長，是最糟的——一個如此沉醉於權力之人，以至於當一座大寺院的僧侶抗議他的政策之時，他把那座寺院燒成了平地。當他自己的兒子警告他，氏族樹敵太多之時，清盛笑了：「如果百姓恨我們，讓他們恨吧。他們能做什麼？」傳統標記這個為鉸鏈——平氏的驕傲從自信越界到魯莽、從權力越界到毀掉權力之傲慢的那個時刻。

And the enemies gathered. The Minamoto — the Genji — the rival samurai clan that the Taira had defeated but never destroyed, began to rise. And the tradition tells us, with the bell's certainty, that this was inevitable — that the Taira's fall was not brought on by their enemies but by themselves, by their own arrogance, by the very pride that made them great. The Dao turns. Yin becomes yang. The mighty fall. And the Taira, who had been the rising force, became, by their own hand, the falling one. The Genji rose. And the war came.

而敵人聚集了。源氏——那個平氏曾擊敗卻從未消滅的敵對武士氏族——開始升起。而傳統以鐘的確定性告訴我們，這是不可避免的——平氏之傾不是被它的敵人所帶來，而是被它自己、被它自己的傲慢、被那份使他們偉大的驕傲本身。道轉。陰成陽。猛者亡。而平氏，那個曾經是升起之力的，由他們自己的手，成了那個下墜的。源氏升起。而戰爭來了。

Chapter 4 · 第四章

The Genji Rise

源氏之起

And the Genji rose, led by Minamoto no Yoritomo and his brilliant half-brother Yoshitsune — and Yoshitsune is, the tradition tells us, one of the great tragic figures of Japanese history. He was a military genius, the commander who won every battle, the hero who broke the Taira's power. And he was also, the tradition tells us, doomed — because his brother Yoritomo, the political mind, feared the half-brother whose military fame outshone his own, and would, in the end, order Yoshitsune's death. The Genji war was not merely a war between clans; it was a war that destroyed the very heroes who won it.

而源氏升起，由源賴朝和他那才華橫溢的同父異母弟弟義經率領——而義經是，傳統告訴我們，日本歷史上最偉大的悲劇人物之一。他是個軍事天才，那位贏了每一場戰役的統帥，那位粉碎了平氏力量的英雄。而他也是，傳統告訴我們，註定毀滅——因為他的哥哥賴朝，那個政治頭腦，畏懼那個軍事名聲蓋過他自己的同父異母弟弟，而終將下令義經之死。源氏之戰不僅僅是一場氏族之間的戰爭；它是一場毀掉了贏得它的英雄本身的戰爭。

And the tradition gives us the great battles of the Genji War — the campaign that rolled the Taira back from Kyoto to the Inland Sea, battle by battle, as the once-mighty clan was driven toward the water where their story would end. And the tradition tells us, with the bell ringing under every page, that each victory was also a loss — because each battle brought the story closer to its end, and the end was the whole point. The Genji won. The Taira fled. And the fleeing clan, reduced now to a remnant, carrying their child-emperor, sailed out onto the Inland Sea for the last battle. The next chapter tells of that battle — the most famous in Japanese literature — and of the drowning that is the bell's final note.

而傳統給了我們源氏合戰的那些大戰——那場將平氏從京都一路逼回瀨戶內海的戰役，一場接一場，那個曾經強大的氏族被趨向水面、趨向他們的故事將終結之處。而傳統以鐘聲在每一頁之下迴響的方式告訴我們，每一場勝利也都是一場失去——因為每一場戰都使故事更接近它的終局，而終局便是全部的重點。源氏贏了。平氏逃了。而那逃亡的氏族，如今只剩殘部，帶著他們的幼帝，駛出瀨戶內海，迎接最後一戰。下一章講述那場戰——日本文學中最著名的——以及那場溺亡，那口鐘的最終之音。

Chapter 5 · 第五章

Ichi-no-Tani

一之谷

And the tradition gives us the Battle of Ichi-no-Tani — and the scene that breaks the heart of every Japanese reader. At Ichi-no-Tani, the Genji attacked the Taira from two sides — and among the Taira dead and captured were boys, children, teenagers barely old enough to hold a sword. The most famous of these was Taira no Atsumori — a young warrior, sixteen years old, beautiful, accomplished, carrying a flute — who was cornered on the beach by the Genji warrior Kumagai.

而傳統給了我們一之谷之戰——而那個場面令每一個日本讀者心碎。在一之谷，源氏從兩面夾擊平氏——而在平氏的死者與俘虜之中，有男孩、孩子、剛剛大到能握劍的少年。其中最著名的是平敦盛——一位年輕武士，十六歲，俊美、多才、帶著一支笛——他在海灘上被源氏武士熊谷逼到了絕境。

And Kumagai, the tradition tells us, looked at the boy — and saw his own son. The same age, the same beauty, the same youth about to be cut down. And Kumagai hesitated. He wanted to spare the boy. But the other Genji warriors were coming, and Kumagai knew that if they captured Atsumori, the boy would be humiliated, tortured, killed dishonorably. And so Kumagai, weeping, cut off the boy's head himself — a clean death, an honorable death, the only mercy he could give. And the tradition tells us that Kumagai, after Ichi-no-Tani, renounced the world, became a monk, and spent the rest of his life praying for the boy he had killed. This single scene — a warrior weeping over the boy he must kill, then spending his life praying for him — is the Heike's great image of the cost of war: not the death of enemies, but the death of children. And the flute that Atsumori carried — the mark of his cultivation, his refinement, his youth — became, in the Noh play that bears his name, the symbol of all that was beautiful and lost.

而熊谷，傳統告訴我們，望著那男孩——而看見了他自己的兒子。同樣的年紀，同樣的美，同樣的即將被砍倒的青春。而熊谷猶豫了。他想要饒過那個男孩。但其他源氏武士正在趕來，而熊谷知道，如果他們捉住了敦盛，那個男孩將被羞辱、折磨、不光彩地被殺。於是熊谷，流著淚，親手砍下了那個男孩的頭——一個乾淨的死、一個光榮的死、他所能給予的唯一仁慈。而傳統告訴我們，熊谷在一之谷之後，厭世了，成了僧人，而他餘生都在為他所殺的那個男孩祈禱。這單一的場面——一個武士為他不得不殺的男孩流淚，然後一生為他祈禱——是《平家》關於戰爭之代價的偉大意象：不是敵人之死，而是孩子之死。而敦盛所帶的那支笛——他的教養、他的修養、他的青春之標記——在那部以他為名的能劇中，成了一切美麗而消逝之物的象徵。

Chapter 6 · 第六章

Dan-no-ura

壇之浦

And the final battle came — at Dan-no-ura, in the narrow waters of the Shimonoseki Strait, where the remnant Taira fleet met the Genji fleet for the last time. And the tradition gives us the battle on the water — ships clashing, arrows flying, the sea churning — and then, the moment that is the emotional climax of the entire epic. The child-emperor Antoku — seven years old, the grandson of Kiyomori, the last of the Taira imperial line — was on the Taira flag-ship, in the arms of his grandmother, the Nun of the Second Rank.

而最終之戰來了——在壇之浦，在下關海峽的窄水之中，平氏的殘部艦隊與源氏艦隊最後一次相遇。而傳統給了我們水面上的那場戰——船相撞、箭齊飛、海翻攪——然後，那個作為整部史詩情感高潮的時刻。幼帝安德——七歲，清盛的外孫，平氏皇室之脈的最後一人——在平氏的旗艦上，在他祖母、二位尼的懷中。

And the Taira were losing. The battle turned against them. The ships were sinking. The clan was dying. And the Nun of the Second Rank, holding the seven-year-old emperor in her arms, made the decision that has echoed through Japanese literature ever since. She would not let the child fall into the hands of the enemy. She would not let him be captured, humiliated, displayed as a trophy. She took the child in her arms — and the tradition tells us she spoke to him, gently, as if putting him to sleep: "Come, my lord. The Eastern Sea is the Pure Land. Below the waves is a capital more beautiful than the one above." And she took him in her arms, and the three Imperial Regalia — the sword, the jewel, the mirror — and she stepped off the side of the ship, into the sea. And the child-emperor Antoku, seven years old, the last of the Taira line, sank beneath the waves with his grandmother, and drowned.

而平氏在敗。戰勢轉向了他們。船在沉。氏族在亡。而二位尼，將七歲的天皇抱在懷中，做了那個此後在日本文學中迴響不息的決定。她不肯讓這個孩子落入敵人之手。她不肯讓他被擒、被辱、被當作戰利品展示。她把孩子抱在懷中——而傳統告訴我們她對他說話，溫柔地，如同哄他入睡：「來吧，吾主。東海是淨土。波浪之下，是一個比上方更美的都城。」而她抱著他，帶著三神器——劍、玉、鏡——而她從船邊走下，走進了海。而幼帝安德，七歲，平氏之脈的最後一人，帶著他的祖母，沉入了波浪之下，而溺亡了。

The sword was lost in the sea — the Kusanagi, the Grass-Cutting Sword, the blade from the belly of the dragon, the second of the three Imperial Regalia, sank beneath the waves at Dan-no-ura and was never recovered. (The mirror and the jewel were saved; the sword that survives today is said to be a replica.) The Taira clan was annihilated. The child-emperor was dead. And the tradition tells us that the spirits of the drowned Taira — the women and children and warriors who sank at Dan-no-ura — became crabs: the Heike-gani, the Heike crabs, whose shells still bear the face of a samurai, the mark of the drowned warriors. The tradition, here, does what it does best: it makes the loss physical, permanent, visible. The Taira are gone. But their faces are still on the bottom of the sea, in the shells of the crabs that bear their name. The bell has rung. The Taira have fallen. The child is drowned. And the whole epic has been, from the first line, the slow, beautiful telling of this single, inevitable moment.

劍沉入了海——草薙劍，來自龍腹之刃，三神器之第二，在壇之浦沉入波浪之下，再未被尋回。（鏡與玉被救起；今日所存之劍據說是複製品。）平氏被殲。幼帝已死。而傳統告訴我們，溺亡的平氏之靈——那些在壇之浦沉沒的女子、孩子、戰士——成了蟹：平家蟹，其殼上仍帶著武士的臉，那些溺亡戰士的印記。傳統，在此處，做它最擅長之事：它使那場失去成為物理的、永久的、可見的。平氏不在了。但他們的臉仍在海底，在那些以他們為名的蟹的殼上。鐘響過了。平氏倒了。孩子溺亡了。而整部史詩，從第一行起，便是這單一的、不可避免的時刻的緩慢而美麗的講述。

Chapter 7 · 第七章

The Bell Revisited

鐘聲再響

And after Dan-no-ura, the survivors — the Taira women who were not drowned, the warriors who were not killed — were scattered. Some married into the victors' families. Some became nuns. Some disappeared into the mountains, and the tradition tells us that, even today, there are remote villages in Japan that claim descent from the fleeing Taira. And the survivors who became monks and nuns — the tradition gives us the figure of Kenreimon'in, Kiyomori's own daughter, the mother of the drowned emperor, who took the veil and spent her final years in a remote hut, praying for her son's soul beneath the waves.

而壇之浦之後，倖存者——那些未被溺亡的平氏女子、那些未被殺的戰士——四散了。有些嫁入了勝者之家。有些成了尼。有些消失在山中，而傳統告訴我們，即便今日，日本仍有偏遠的村莊自稱是逃亡平氏的後裔。而那些成為僧尼的倖存者——傳統給了我們建禮門院的形象，清盛親生的女兒，那個溺亡天皇之母，她出家了，而在一座偏遠的草庵裡度過了她最後的歲月，在波浪之下為她兒子的靈魂祈禱。

And the tradition tells us that Kenreimon'in, in her hut, had a dream — a vision of the Pure Land beneath the sea, where her son and her clan, the drowned Taira, were at peace. And the dream, the tradition says, gave her a measure of consolation — not the consolation of reunion, for the dead are gone, but the consolation of knowing that the very impermanence that took them is also the law that governs everything, and that there is no exception, and that the grief is part of the beauty, and the beauty is part of the grief. The bell's final note is not despair. It is acceptance. The Taira are gone. The bell rings still. And the sound of the bell — the sound of impermanence — is, the tradition tells us, the sound of the world being itself. The proud do not endure. They are like a dream on a spring night. And the dream was beautiful. And the beauty was real. And both are true. And both are gone.

而傳統告訴我們，建禮門院，在她的草庵裡，做了一個夢——一個海底淨土的異象，在那裡，她的兒子和她的氏族、那些溺亡的平氏，是安寧的。而那個夢，傳統說，給了她一份慰藉——不是重逢的慰藉，因為死者已逝，而是那個「知道那個帶走他們的無常，也正是治理一切之法、沒有例外、而悲傷是美的一部分、而美是悲傷的一部分」的慰藉。那口鐘的最終之音不是絕望。它是接受。平氏不在了。鐘仍然響著。而那鐘聲——無常之聲——是，傳統告訴我們，世界作為自身之聲。驕者不持久。宛如春夜一夢。而那場夢是美麗的。而那份美是真實的。而兩者皆真。而兩者皆逝。

THE SOUND OF THE BELL

鐘之聲

"The sound of the Gion bells echoes the impermanence of all things." So it began, and so it ends. The Taira rose to absolute power, ruled with arrogance, and were driven back, battle by battle, to the sea. At Ichi-no-Tani, a warrior wept over the boy he had to kill, and spent his life praying for him. At Dan-no-ura, a grandmother took a seven-year-old emperor into the waves, and the sword from a dragon's belly sank beneath the sea. The crabs still carry samurai faces. The bell still rings. And the sound it makes — impermanence, impermanence, impermanence — is the sound of all that is beautiful, passing. The dream was real. The dream is gone. Both are true. And the bell rings on.
