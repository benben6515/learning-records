IMPERMANENCE & THE GARDEN'S END

無　常　與　花　園　之　終

MUJŌ 無常 · THE CHERRY BLOSSOM · NINE CIVILIZATIONS, ONE QUESTION

The East Asian answer to the question every civilization in this garden has been asking — from Gilgamesh at the world's edge to Arthur sleeping in Avalon, from Sita walking into the earth to the Heike drowning in the sea — is the single word mujō: impermanence. All things pass. The beautiful is beautiful because it fades. And the only permanence is change itself. This is the final stage of the final civilization — and it is the place where all nine gardens meet, and where the whole vast collection turns, at last, to look at itself. 東亞對這座花園中每一個文明一直在問的那個問題的答案——從世界盡頭的吉爾伽美什到沉睡於阿瓦隆的亞瑟、從走入大地的悉多到沉於大海的平家——是單一一個詞：無常。萬物皆逝。美因其消逝而美。而唯一的永恆是變易本身。這是最後一個文明的最後一個階段——而它是九座花園相遇之處，也是整個浩瀚的集子最終轉身回望自身之處。

Chapter 1 · 第一章

Mujō — The Law of Impermanence

無常

Mujō. The word means, simply, "no-permanence" — the absence of anything that stays. And it is the deepest teaching the East Asian traditions share, and the one that most sets them apart from the other gardens in this collection. In Greece, the heroes seek kleos — a fame that outlasts the body, a permanence achieved through being remembered. In Mesopotamia, Gilgamesh learns that the only immortality is the work of your hands — the walls that stand. In India, the Gita teaches that the Self was never born and can never die — a permanence beyond the body. But in East Asia, the teaching cuts the other way: nothing is permanent. Not the body. Not the fame. Not the walls. Not the Self as you experience it. Everything — without exception — is flowing, changing, passing.

無常。這個詞的意思，簡單地，是「沒有-永恆」——任何停留之物的缺席。而它是東亞傳統所共享的最深教誨，也是使它們與這個集子中其他花園最為不同的那一個。在希臘，英雄尋求 kleos——一份比身體更長壽的名聲、一份透過被記住而達成的永恆。在兩河流域，吉爾伽美什學到唯一的永生是你雙手的作為——那些矗立的城牆。在印度，《薄伽梵歌》教導真我從未出生、也永不死去——一份超越身體的永恆。但在東亞，教誨切向另一個方向：沒有什麼是永恆的。不是身體。不是名聲。不是城牆。不是你所體驗的那個自我。一切——無一例外——都在流動、變化、消逝。

And the East Asian tradition is not grieved by this — or rather, it is grieved, and it finds the grief itself to be the point. The beauty of the cherry blossom is inseparable from its falling. The beauty of the morning is inseparable from its passing. The beauty of a great clan is inseparable from its decline. And to love the beauty — to truly love it — is to love its passing too, because the passing is what makes it beautiful. This is mujō — not a lament, but a way of seeing: a way of holding every beautiful thing lightly, of knowing it is already going, and of finding, in that knowledge, not despair but a deeper, more tender love. The bell rings. All things pass. And the sound of the passing is the sound of the world being real.

而東亞的傳統不為此悲——或者更確切地說，它悲，而它發現那份悲本身就是重點。櫻花的美與它的凋零不可分割。清晨的美與它的消逝不可分割。一個大族的美與它的衰亡不可分割。而愛那份美——真正地愛它——便是也愛它的消逝，因為那份消逝正是使它美的東西。這便是無常——不是一首輓歌，而是一種看的方式：一種輕輕地執著於每一件美麗之物、知道它已在離去、而在那份知曉中找到的不是絕望、而是一份更深、更柔之愛的方式。鐘響。萬物皆逝。而消逝之聲，是世界成為真實之聲。

Chapter 2 · 第二章

The Cherry Blossom

櫻花

And the image of mujō — the image that carries the whole teaching in a single visible thing — is the cherry blossom. The sakura blooms in spring, brilliant, covering the hills in clouds of pink and white, and then — within days, within a single week — it falls. The petals scatter. The ground is covered. The trees are bare again. And the Japanese tradition has made this brief, brilliant flowering-and-falling the symbol of everything mujō teaches: that the most beautiful things are the most transient, that perfection exists only at the moment of its passing, that the cherry tree is most beautiful not when it blooms but when the blossoms fall.

而無常的意象——那個以一件可見之物承載整個教誨的意象——是櫻花。櫻花在春天綻放，燦爛，以粉白之雲覆蓋山丘，然後——在數日之內、在一個星期之內——它落了。花瓣散了。地上覆滿了。樹又空了。而日本傳統將這短暫而燦爛的綻放與凋落，做成了無常所教的一切之象徵：最美之物最為短暫，完美只存在於其消逝之那一刻，櫻樹最美之時不是花開，而是花落。

This is the image that governs Japanese aesthetics — the aesthetics of wabi-sabi, of the beauty of the impermanent, the incomplete, the fading. The tea ceremony, with its rough bowl and its deliberate simplicity. The garden, with its single stone and its empty space. The poem, seventeen syllables, catching a single moment of perception before it dissolves. All of these are practices of mujō — ways of training the eye to see the beauty in the passing, and to find, in that passing, the deepest kind of meaning. For the East Asian tradition, a thing does not need to last to be meaningful. A thing is meaningful BECAUSE it does not last. The cherry blossom teaches this. The Heike teaches this. And the whole vast East Asian imagination, from the Dao to the kamikaze pilot's last poem, has been shaped by it. Hold it lightly. It is already falling. And the falling is the flower.

這是那個治理日本美學的意象——侘寂之美學、無常之美、不完整之美、褪色之美。茶道，帶著它粗獷的碗和它刻意的簡素。庭園，帶著它的一塊石和它的空白。俳句，十七音，捕捉一個在消融之前的單一感知之瞬。所有這些都是無常的修行——訓練眼去看消逝中的美、而在那份消逝中找到最深之意義的方式。對東亞的傳統而言，一物不需要持久才有意義。一物有意義，正因為它不持久。櫻花教導了這一點。《平家》教導了這一點。而整個浩瀚的東亞想像，從道到神風特攻隊的最後一首詩，都被它所塑造。輕輕地執著。它已在落了。而那場落，便是花。

Chapter 3 · 第三章

The Journey & the Wave

旅程與浪潮

And here, at the end of the East Asian cycle, the two traditions within it stand side by side, and we can see them whole. The Chinese Journey to the West — the story of a monkey who rebelled, was crushed, and walked a long road to becoming a Buddha — teaches persistence, redemption, the slow transformation of power into wisdom. The Japanese Tale of the Heike — the story of a clan that rose and fell and drowned — teaches impermanence, the beauty of the passing, the bell that rings beneath everything.

而在這裡，在東亞循環的終點，其內的兩個傳統並肩而立，而我們可以完整地看見它們。中國的《西遊記》——那個反叛、被鎮、走過漫長之路成為佛的猴子的故事——教導堅毅、救贖、力量向智慧的緩慢轉化。日本的《平家物語》——那個升起、衰落、溺亡的氏族的故事——教導無常、消逝之美、那口在一切之下迴響的鐘。

And the two teachings are not contradictory — they are complementary. The Journey says: walk the road, do not give up, the transformation is real, the destination is worth reaching. The Heike says: and when you reach it, hold it lightly, because it too will pass. The Journey says: there is meaning in the effort. The Heike says: and the meaning is inseparable from its passing. Together, they form the complete East Asian answer to the question every civilization in this garden has been asking: how do you live, knowing that everything — including yourself — is going? You walk the road (the Chinese answer). And you love the falling petals (the Japanese answer). And you do both at once. And the doing of both — the walking and the letting-go, the effort and the acceptance — is, the tradition tells us, the deepest wisdom any of these stories have to offer.

而這兩個教誨並不矛盾——它們是互補的。《西遊記》說：走那條路，不要放棄，轉化是真實的，目的地值得一到。《平家》說：而當你到了，輕輕地執著，因為它也將消逝。《西遊記》說：努力中有意義。《平家》說：而那份意義與其消逝不可分割。兩者一起，它們構成了東亞對這座花園中每一個文明一直在問的那個問題的完整答案：你如何活著，知道一切——包括你自己——都在離去？你走那條路（中國的答案）。而你愛那些凋落的花瓣（日本的答案）。而你兩者同時做。而那個兩者皆做——行走與放下、努力與接受——是，傳統告訴我們，這些故事中任何一個所能提供的最深智慧。

Chapter 4 · 第四章

The Modern Echoes

現代的迴響

And the stories live. The Journey to the West is, the tradition tells us, the most adapted story in the world — from Dragon Ball (whose hero, Son Goku, is Sun Wukong in a different form) to countless films, games, anime, and operas. Every Chinese child knows the Monkey King. Every Japanese child knows the Tale of the Heike's opening lines. And the kami of the Kojiki are still worshipped — at Ise, at Izumo, at the tens of thousands of shrines that dot the Japanese landscape. The myths did not die. They became the fabric of the culture — so deeply woven in that a Japanese person cannot eat a bowl of noodles or walk past a shrine or watch a cherry tree bloom without touching, however unconsciously, the stories this cycle has told.

而那些故事活著。《西遊記》是，傳統告訴我們，世界上被改編最多的故事——從《七龍珠》（其英雄孫悟空，便是另一種形式的孫悟空）到無數的電影、遊戲、動漫、戲曲。每一個中國孩子都認識猴王。每一個日本孩子都知道《平家物語》的開卷之辭。而《古事記》中的神仍被崇拜——在伊勢、在出雲、在遍布日本風景的數萬座神社之中。那些神話沒有死。它們成了文化的經緯——織入得如此之深，以至於一個日本人吃一碗麵、或走過一座神社、或看一棵櫻樹綻放，都不能不——無論多麼無意識地——觸碰到這個循環所講述的那些故事。

And the Heike crabs still carry samurai faces on their shells. And the cherry trees still bloom and fall. And the bell of the Gion monastery, if it rings at all, rings in the minds of everyone who has ever read the opening line. The East Asian myths are not museum pieces. They are living presences — in the manga, in the anime, in the tea ceremony, in the garden, in the shrine, in the way a person bows, in the way a person grieves, in the way a person looks at a flower and sees, in its falling, the whole of what it means to be alive. The stories endure. Not because they are permanent — nothing is. But because they are told. And told again. And told again. The telling is the only permanence there is.

而平家蟹的殼上仍帶著武士的臉。而櫻樹仍在綻放與凋落。而祇園精舍的那口鐘，如果它還響，響在每一個曾經讀過那句開卷之辭的人心中。東亞的神話不是博物館裡的展品。它們是活的臨在——在漫畫裡、在動漫裡、在茶道裡、在庭園裡、在神社裡、在一個人鞠躬的方式裡、在一個人悲慟的方式裡、在一個人看著一朵花而在其凋落之中看見活著的全部意義的方式裡。那些故事持久。不是因為它們是永恆的——沒有什麼是。而是因為它們被講述。而又講述。而又講述。講述，是唯一的永恆。

Chapter 5 · 第五章

The Garden Closes

花園收卷

And now the whole garden turns, at last, to look at itself. Nine civilizations. Nine answers to the same questions. Where did the world come from? A giant's body, a duck's egg, a dragon's corpse, a maiden on the sea, the word of a god, a stone monkey's birth. Why must we die? Because a goddess was forced to race, because a serpent offered fruit, because a boy's curiosity opened a box, because the Buddha's law says all things pass. What should we do with the time we have? Seek glory (the Greeks). Build walls (the Mesopotamians). Defy doom (the Norse). Serve the realm (the Persians). Do our duty (the Indians). Try to build a just court (the Arthurian). Keep the song alive (the Kalevala). Hold the ford (the Celts). Walk the road, and love the falling petals (the East Asians).

而如今整座花園終於轉身回望自身。九個文明。對同樣的問題的九個答案。世界從何而來？一個巨人的身體、一顆鴨蛋、一條龍的屍骸、海上的一位少女、一個神的話語、一隻石猴的誕生。我們為何必須死去？因為一位女神被強迫去賽跑、因為一條蛇獻了果、因為一個男孩的好奇打開了一個盒子、因為佛祖的法說萬物皆逝。我們該如何度過我們所有的時間？追尋榮光（希臘人）。建造城牆（兩河流域人）。對抗毀滅（北歐人）。服務國度（波斯人）。盡我們的責任（印度人）。嘗試建造一個公正的宮廷（亞瑟王的人）。保持歌的活著（卡勒瓦拉的人）。守住渡口（凱爾特人）。走那條路，並愛那些凋落的花瓣（東亞人）。

And each answer is true — and none is complete. The Greeks sought glory, and Achilles wept. The Mesopotamians built walls, and the walls fell. The Norse defied doom, and the doom came. The East Asians love the falling petals, and the petals fall. No civilization in this garden has the whole answer. Each has a piece — a true piece, a beautiful piece — and the garden exists to set the pieces side by side, and to let the reader see, in the comparison, the shape of something none of them can see alone. The garden is not an argument for one answer over another. It is an argument that the question is worth asking — that the asking itself, across nine civilizations and three thousand years, is the thing that makes us human. The bell rings. The petals fall. The monkey walks. The sun comes out of the cave. And somewhere, in every age, in every language, a storyteller sits down and begins: "Once, in the beginning..."

而每一個答案都是真的——而沒有一個是完整的。希臘人追尋榮光，而阿基里斯哭了。兩河流域人建造城牆，而城牆倒了。北歐人對抗毀滅，而毀滅來了。東亞人愛凋落的花瓣，而花瓣落了。這座花園中沒有一個文明擁有完整的答案。每一個都有一塊——一塊真實的塊、一塊美麗的塊——而花園的存在，是為了把那些塊並排放在一起，讓讀者在比較中看見，那個他們之中沒有任何一個能獨自看見之物的形。花園不是一個主張一個答案優於另一個的論證。它是一個「那個問題值得問」的論證——那個「問」本身，跨越九個文明、三千年，是使我們成為人的那件事。鐘響。花瓣落。猴子走。太陽出洞。而在某處，在每一個時代、在每一種語言裡，一個說故事的人坐下來，開始：「很久很久以前，在太初之時……」

THE GARDEN IS COMPLETE

花園，已成

Nine civilizations. Three hundred and sixty chapters. The same questions, asked in nine languages, across three thousand years. And the East Asian answer — mujō, impermanence — is the final voice in the chorus: the bell that rings beneath everything, the cherry blossom that is most beautiful in its falling, the road that is worth walking precisely because it does not go on forever. The Greeks sought glory. The Norse defied doom. The Indians did their duty. The East Asians loved the passing. And all of them — every one — were telling the truth. The garden is complete. The bell rings. The petals fall. And somewhere, a storyteller sits down to begin again.
